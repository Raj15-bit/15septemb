The oil shock has turned into a shortage, and the cost of money is rising with it

Closed sea routes and cancelled Saudi cargoes are pushing fuel prices up, central banks towards rate hikes and bond yields to record highs. In India, a new UPI fee, a forced listing for Tata Sons and a crowded IPO calendar meet a market that has just had its worst day in months.

01

The oil shock has become a shortage

The main problem is no longer the price of oil. It is whether buyers can get the oil at all.

The Houthis, an armed group in Yemen, now hold the whole Red Sea coast of Yemen. That gives them control of the Bab el-Mandeb, the narrow sea channel at the south end of the Red Sea. Before the war with Iran, over 9 million barrels of crude a day passed through it, about 9% of world oil flows. Iran's Revolutionary Guard also says a supertanker blew up after hitting a mine and that the Strait of Hormuz, the channel between Iran and Oman that carries most Gulf oil, is closed.

Saudi Arabia had a way around Hormuz: the East-West pipeline, which carries Saudi crude across the country to the Red Sea port of Yanbu. After repeated attacks, Saudi Arabia shut that pipeline. It then told some European refiners that their September crude cargoes are cancelled. Until now the kingdom kept its delivery promises by drawing on oil it keeps in storage tanks around the world. Cancelling cargoes means that stored cushion is running out, so the shortage is now reaching real buyers.

The prices show 2 different stories. US crude traded near $103 a barrel, and Brent, the main world benchmark, near $106. But the crude contract traded in Shanghai hit $125 and then a record $135. That price is for oil delivered to China. A gap of $18 or more over Brent is the widest in the past 12 months, which means Asian buyers are paying far more for barrels that can actually be shipped, while futures in London and New York are held down by hopes of a peace deal.

Those hopes are thin. US President Donald Trump said Iran wants a deal and that oil prices will "drop like a rock" once fighting ends. The Dow Jones index wiped out its losses on that remark. About 40 minutes later, Iranian state media rejected his claim. Oil kept rising.

The shock is spreading through fuel products first. US diesel traded near $4.96 a gallon. US jet fuel rose 10% in 1 week and 61% in 10 weeks, and US airfares were already 23% higher in August than a year earlier, before most of the oil jump showed up. Senate leader John Thune said he is open to banning US diesel exports. The US is the largest diesel exporter and Russia, the 2nd largest, has already stopped. A US ban would push diesel prices up everywhere else.

From fuel, the shock moves into prices in general, then into interest rates. In India, retail inflation rose to 4.82%, the highest in 20 months. On 15 September the Sensex fell 778 points, the Nifty closed at a 5-month low and more than 900 stocks fell 3% or more, the first such day since May. Only IT stocks such as HCL Technologies (+4%), Infosys (+3.7%) and TCS (+2.3%) rose. Reliance Industries hit a 17-month low. So a war fought far away is now setting the cost of money in India, which is why the rest of this issue keeps coming back to oil.

02

Hard limits that money cannot rush

Power equipment, energy storage and factory space are all full, and no amount of spending shortens the wait.

Power for AI is now the thing that decides revenue

Figures from Bloom Energy put the earning power of a 1 gigawatt AI data centre campus (1 gigawatt is 1,000 megawatts) at $12 to 24 billion a year, at a 40% to 50% gross margin. So getting electricity 1 month earlier is worth $1 to 2 billion to the owner. Power has stopped being a cost line and has become the gate on revenue, which is why operators now pay almost any price for power equipment.

The suppliers are full. INNIO, which makes gas engines, took a record $2.3 billion of orders, up 316%, and says it is sold out for 2026 and 2027. Caterpillar is adding price-rise clauses to generator sets for 2029 delivery. GE Vernova has 116 gigawatts of gas turbines on order and is lifting output only from 20 to 30 gigawatts a year by 2030. Siemens Energy booked 15 gigawatts in a single quarter. Both turbine makers are held back by the same "hot-section" castings and forgings, the heat-proof metal parts that also limit jet engine output.

A turbine maker that cannot make more turbines hands out everything else: the generator, the piping, the enclosure and the skid (the steel base frame the machine sits on). That is where India comes in. TD Power Systems is set to become a build-to-print generator factory for Siemens Energy, meaning it builds to Siemens drawings, carries no design risk, earns a thinner margin and gets much higher volume. But nothing enters its books until purchase orders arrive, and at 18 to 20 months of build time the Siemens work is an FY29 event, so the orders matter more than the announcement. The stock still fell 9.86% on 15 September with other popular names.

In the US, data centres are set to burn more natural gas than most countries. The US Environmental Protection Agency plans to scrap carbon limits on power plants, while a court rejected an Energy Department order to keep a Michigan coal plant running. So the US is choosing more fossil power to feed AI, which ties AI demand directly to the gas and oil prices in section 01.

India's storage gap

India wants 500 gigawatts of non-fossil power by 2030, but solar only produces in the day. Pumped storage fixes that with 2 water reservoirs at least 150 to 200 metres apart in height: surplus power pumps water up, and at peak time the water runs down through turbines. India has 87 such projects planned, totalling 122.77 gigawatts, which need about ₹7.36 trillion at roughly ₹6 crore per megawatt. Only 7.42 gigawatts work today, including the 1,000 megawatt Tehri plant.

Batteries cost about ₹2 crore per megawatt, a third of pumped storage, but usually discharge for up to 4 hours and last about a tenth as long. Pumped storage runs for 6 hours or more and lasts about 100 years, yet takes up to 5 years to build. The need is urgent: 43 gigawatts of solar capacity still has no buyer, and power distribution companies are selling green power on exchanges below what they paid. The grid must also hold its frequency between 49.90 and 50.05 hertz, which gets harder as more solar and wind are added. Tata Power, Greenko, Mahagenco and TANGEDCO are among the few that have pumped storage running.

Factories running flat out

Waiting time for popular electric scooters has stretched to 2 months from about 2 weeks. Ather Energy said supply shortages were costing it 13,000 to 15,000 scooter sales a month, which is why it is building a plant in Aurangabad for up to 10 lakh units a year. In glass-lined equipment (steel tanks coated with glass, used by chemical and drug makers), GMM Pfaudler says its India plants went from 50-60% use to full capacity. When plants are full, the next rupee of sales needs new capex, so the order books in section 06 need to be read together with how much room each company has left.

03

Money is getting dearer everywhere

Central banks are turning to rate hikes, bond buyers are stepping back, and India is dealing with too many dollars at once.

The US Federal Reserve is expected to raise rates

The US Federal Reserve, now led by Kevin Warsh, announces its decision on 16 September. Goldman Sachs now expects a rate hike, and says this is not because the economy needs it but because Warsh does not want to let the market down. Betting markets put the odds of a 0.25 percentage point hike at about 85%. Albert Edwards of Société Générale called a hike "criminally stupid". The European Central Bank has already raised rates, and the Bank of Japan is expected to follow. Kevin Hassett said he and Trump will respect whatever Warsh decides. So the world is entering a new round of rate hikes, likely a small one, set off by oil and not by strong growth.

Bond buyers are pulling back

A bond yield is the yearly return a lender demands for lending money for that long. When buyers are fewer, the government must offer a higher yield. The US sold 20-year bonds at 5.42%, the highest ever for that bond. The auction also had a "tail" of 0.02 percentage points, meaning the government had to pay more than traders expected just before the sale, the biggest miss since December 2024. Indirect bidders, which mostly means foreign buyers, took only 52.5%, the lowest on record. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had said the US just held 2 of its most successful debt auctions in 20 years. Clients of JPMorgan are adding bets that bond prices will fall.

Long-dated US government bonds have lost about 50% of their value since 2020, so banks, pension funds and insurers are holding large paper losses. Those losses only turn real if the bonds have to be sold, which is why a forced sale is the risk people are watching. The same pressure is visible in Britain and Europe. The European Central Bank has widened a facility that lets foreign central banks pledge euro bonds and get fresh euros, instead of selling the bonds. That keeps sellers out of the market, and some read it as an early step towards yield curve control, where a central bank caps long-term yields by printing money to buy bonds.

US households feel it through mortgages

The average 30-year US home loan rate hit 7.22%, over 1 percentage point above its low on 23 February, the week before the Iran war began. On a $500,000 loan, the monthly payment rose from about $3,000 to about $3,400, or $4,800 more a year. Buyers are giving up. In August there were 57.9% more home sellers than buyers, the biggest gap on record: about 1,534,918 sellers against about 972,300 buyers. 36 of the 49 largest US metro areas are now buyer's markets, led by Nashville (+139%), Miami (+138%) and Houston (+131%).

India has the opposite problem: too many dollars

The Reserve Bank of India ran a special swap window from 8 June that drew $136.37 billion, far more than expected. Of this, $127.22 billion came as FCNR(B) deposits, foreign currency deposits kept by Indians abroad, mostly for 5 years. This figure was checked against RBI data reported by Business Standard. India's foreign exchange reserves jumped $45 billion in 1 week to $786 billion, overtaking Russia ($754 billion) to rank 4th in the world after China, Japan and Switzerland.

But every dollar the RBI takes in puts rupees into banks. The surplus is estimated at ₹4.3 to 4.7 trillion, and the RBI wants to pull it back. It announced ₹1 trillion of open market sales, where it sells government bonds to soak up cash, plus a ₹50,000 crore government bond sale on 17 September. More bonds on sale means lower bond prices, so Indian bond yields rose and long-dated bond funds did worst while liquid funds did best. The rupee also gave back most of the gain it made after the dollar inflow. UBS strategist Bhanu Baweja says 100 rupees to the dollar is possible within 12 to 18 months. The RBI's repo rate is 5.25%, and its October policy meeting is now a live chance for a hike, which would line up its rate stance with its effort to drain cash.

Gold keeps moving east

China is reported to be buying about twice as much gold as it officially declares. In India, Muthoot Finance holds nearly 200 tonnes of gold pledged by borrowers. That is about as much as Singapore's central bank holds, more than Brazil's (about 175 tonnes) and about 3 times Pakistan's (about 65 tonnes). Muthoot does not own this gold, but the size shows how much household gold now backs Indian loans.

Fuel, trade and who ends up paying

Energy is rising while metals and farm goods fall, and new trade deals do not turn into exports on their own.

Only energy is going up

In the latest weekly market data, US crude (WTI) rose 9.3%. Almost everything else fell: silver 2.6%, steel 1.9%, copper 1.3%, aluminium 1.1% and wheat 1.1%. Equities, bonds, the dollar, crypto and gold all ended lower too; the only notable gainers were crude and Korean shares. So this is not a broad commodity boom. It is an energy squeeze, and falling metal prices point to weaker factory demand at the same time, which is a bad mix for company profits.

Diesel is the product to watch

Indian refineries are running at up to 108% of their rated capacity because diesel demand is so strong. That is possible for short spells when plants push past their design limits. With Russia no longer exporting diesel and the US thinking about a ban, Indian refiners are selling into a short world market, which helps their margins. A US ban would also raise diesel costs for importers such as Japan, which could then sell US government bonds to pay for fuel. That would push US yields even higher, so the diesel market and the bond market in section 03 are tied together.

Fuel costs are reaching shops and flights. US convenience stores are changing what they stock as gas and diesel prices jump. US airfares were 23% higher in August than a year before, and the September figure could show a rise near 30% because most of the jet fuel jump has not yet been counted.

India-EU trade deal: the quota is there, the cars are not

The India-EU free trade agreement, concluded on 27 January 2026, now has its detailed tariff tables. For cars, it sets these limits:

Yet Škoda Auto Volkswagen India has ruled out sending its India-built Kylaq SUV to Europe. As recently as June, Škoda chief Klaus Zellmer called the idea "very much on the table". So even the most obvious candidate chose not to use the quota, which means the deal gives access but does not create demand for Indian cars in Europe by itself. Separately, Trump said he would be fine with Chinese carmakers building cars in the US.

Phones: India now makes almost all it buys

99.2% of phones sold in India are now made in India, against 26% in 2014-15. Phone production grew from ₹18,900 crore in FY14 to ₹4.22 lakh crore in FY24, and exports are above ₹1.29 lakh crore. Uttar Pradesh makes about 65% of India's phones, mostly in the Noida and Yamuna Expressway belt, where Samsung, Oppo, Vivo, Dixon Technologies, Optiemus Infracom, Padget Electronics and Zetwerk have plants. The new ₹62,500 crore Mobile Phone Manufacturing Scheme (FY27 to FY31) pays for actual output and pushes makers to produce parts such as displays, camera units and circuit boards here, and to build Indian-owned designs and brands. Assembly was the easy part; the next stage is where most of the value sits.

Clothes: thin margins in China, scale in Tiruppur

Chinese clothing makers with yearly revenue above $2.98 million earned $84 billion in revenue from January to July but only $2.51 billion in profit, a margin of about 3%, and revenue is falling. With so little profit left, they have little room to cut prices further. Tiruppur in Tamil Nadu has grown from a ₹429 crore cluster in 1991 to ₹75,000 crore of turnover, with ₹46,000 crore of exports to over 50 countries and about 10 lakh jobs, 75% of them held by women. About 80% of its units are small specialist firms, doing only knitting, dyeing, printing or packing, rather than big all-in-one factories. It recycles about 130 million litres of water a day and aims for ₹1 lakh crore of exports by 2030 by moving into man-made fibres and using the UK and EU trade deals.

05

India: rules that moved prices

A new UPI fee, a forced listing for Tata Sons and a strong economy that the stock market chose to ignore.

UPI gets a fee, but not for you

The National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), which runs UPI, will charge a merchant discount rate (MDR) on some shop payments from 15 October. MDR is a small cut of the bill that the shop pays to the payment system. Sending money to a person stays free for any amount, and paying a shop stays free up to ₹2,000. Above that, the shop pays 0.4%, capped at ₹300. These basic terms were checked against news reports on NPCI's notice.

Apps cannot add a platform fee, there is no monthly limit on free payments, and banks have been "advised" to make sure shops do not pass the charge to buyers. Advised is not the same as banned, so whether a 0.4% line quietly appears on bills at jewellers or electronics shops will only be known once the fee starts.

How much money moves? Payments above ₹2,000 are only about 4% of shop payments by count but about 66% of shop payment value. Person-to-person transfers are about 70% of all UPI value and stay free. Put together, about 19.5% of all UPI value falls in the fee zone, a figure reported by Business Today for FY26 that matches the other 2 numbers (30% of value is shop payments, and 66% of that is 19.8%). The flat ₹5 group alone is 17% of shop payment count and 46% of shop payment value, so a large part of the fee zone pays almost nothing. The fee pool is therefore modest.

Both sides have a case. BharatPe co-founder Ashneer Grover says handling cash and ATMs costs India about ₹30,500 crore a year, so a charge on digital payments could push some shops back to cash. The other side says UPI has real costs for technology, security and fraud checks. The government spent about ₹8,700 crore from FY22 to FY25 to keep UPI free for shops, and NPCI itself held ₹6,119 crore of cash, made about ₹1,900 crore of pre-tax operating profit and paid about ₹1,000 crore in tax. The clearest winner is Paytm. Jefferies raised its Paytm earnings estimates by 20% to 25% while assuming only a 0.25% fee, and the fee could add ₹400 to 500 crore to Paytm's FY28 EBITDA because the extra revenue has almost no extra cost. Paytm shares posted their highest weekly close ever.

Tata Sons is being pushed to list

In a letter dated 11 September, the RBI rejected Tata Sons' request to give up its registration as a non-bank finance company. Tata Sons is classed as an upper-layer NBFC, a group of large finance companies that must list their shares. Tata Trusts, led by Noel Tata, own about 66% and oppose listing. The Shapoorji Pallonji Group owns about 18.4% and wants a listing, because it would finally let it sell. These details were checked against The Tribune's report, which also says chairman N Chandrasekaran will not seek another term when his current one ends in February 2027. Estimates put Tata Sons' value at ₹9 to 12.5 lakh crore.

Tata Chemicals shares hit their 20% upper limit because it owns about 2.5% of Tata Sons while its own market value was about ₹15,000 crore. At those estimates, 2.5% of Tata Sons is worth ₹22,500 to 31,250 crore, which is more than the whole of Tata Chemicals was valued at, so the market repriced the stake.

Credit to large industries grew 18% in July, and direct benefit transfers to citizens have reached ₹2.08 trillion so far in FY27. Yet the market fell to a 5-month low. The reason is that share prices are being set by oil, bond yields, the risk of an RBI hike and heavy IPO supply, not by growth data. Rules also hit trading volumes: from April 2026, securities transaction tax on futures rose from 0.02% to 0.05% and on option selling from 0.10% to 0.15%.

A widely shared claim said India spends 25% of government revenue on interest. Budget data shows the Centre plans ₹14.04 lakh crore of interest in FY27, about 25% of total spending. Since the government borrows to fund part of that spending, interest takes a larger share of revenue than of spending, so the 25% figure understates the load when read against revenue.

Other steps the government took

The telecom department backed a 5% revenue share for satellite spectrum, clearing the way for Starlink, Jio and Airtel to sell satellite internet. Jio and Airtel are now partnering with satellite firms and acting as their local sellers. Satellite internet suits border areas, ships and flights, but costly equipment and limited bandwidth mean it cannot beat cheap city fibre. Railway Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw opened 4 Gati Shakti cargo terminals in Gujarat to move port cargo north; ₹5,600 crore of rail work is under way in Kachchh, and the Vapi-Surat bullet train stretch targets December 2026. The Indian Navy invited bids for 7 Project 17B stealth frigates, with the shipyard part worth about ₹49,000 crore.

Hindustan Aeronautics (HAL) will hand over the first 3 HTT-40 trainer aircraft under a ₹6,838 crore order for 70 planes, plus a civil-certified Dhruv NG helicopter and an LCA trainer. Honeywell delivered the first engines for the HTT-40 in June under a deal for 88 units, and HAL now makes 20 HTT-40s a year on 2 lines. Critics say HAL should talk less about side projects until it delivers the LCA Mk1A fighter, now that engine supply is no longer the excuse. Bharat Forge and Pratt & Whitney Canada will study fitting turboprop engines to a DRDO high-altitude, long-endurance drone.

06

India: where the value really sits

In several cases the quick answer and the careful answer point in different directions.

NSE: a monopoly that is losing share

Shares of the National Stock Exchange (NSE) start trading on 24 September, but on BSE, because an exchange cannot list on itself. The IPO price band is ₹1,700 to 1,785. At the top, NSE is valued at ₹4.42 lakh crore, which is 42.9 times FY26 earnings. The whole issue is an offer for sale, so NSE gets no money; only existing owners sell, and they sell just 5.11% of the company. Ashishkumar Chauhan, who took BSE public in 2017 as its chief, is now taking NSE public.

The weak spot is options, which bring in 60.22% of NSE's operating revenue. In 2024 the market regulator SEBI allowed each exchange only 1 weekly expiry day. NSE took Tuesday and BSE took Thursday from September 2025, and traders stayed with Thursday, the day markets had used for decades. NSE's share of equity options (by premium traded) has fallen by 28 points in 2 years.

At first sight NSE looks about 23% cheaper than BSE. But about a fifth of NSE's pre-tax profit is interest on its own cash pile, and nobody pays 43 times earnings for a bond portfolio. Busier factories, same revenue

HLE Glascoat shipped 30% to 40% more glass-lined equipment in India over about 4 years, yet revenue stayed around ₹200 to 250 crore, because the value of each machine fell. Plant use is now 75%, up from 60-65%, but that alone does not prove a recovery. The measure to watch is value per machine and the share of premium products, where its Thaletec range is now about 20%. GMM Pfaudler shows the other path: it won 2 large orders for peptide-making systems, is getting orders from semiconductor, nuclear, defence and mining clients, says 90% of the world's top chemical companies use its equipment, and its Vatva plant can build units weighing 350 tonnes. So 2 companies in the same trade can have very different revenue quality, and unit counts alone hide that.

Rentomojo: the asset keeps earning

Rentomojo, which rents furniture and appliances, grew revenue from ₹193 crore in FY24 to ₹387 crore in FY26, and profit from ₹22 crore to ₹104 crore, with a 41.5% EBITDA margin. Live subscribers rose from 1.49 lakh to 2.54 lakh, repeat orders rose to 50.4% and occupancy is about 83%. The key fact: over 56% of items bought in FY17 were still earning rent in FY26 and had already earned 5.1 times their cost. It refurbished 6.17 lakh items in FY26 to rent them again. So the number to watch is revenue per rupee of assets and how many rental cycles each item completes, because that is how the business could grow faster than its capital base.

Laurus Labs: 2 clocks

Laurus Labs CFO V V Ravi Kumar won a CFO of the Year award. In April 2023, a Nuvama analyst questioned its contract manufacturing (CDMO) plans and heavy capex sitting at 50-60% use. FY24 then missed: revenue fell 17% and EBITDA margin was 15.8%, below the 20-21% floor management had talked about. But the company kept investing, including about ₹800 crore in synthesis capacity, and expensed start-up costs instead of capitalising them. By FY26 revenue was ₹6,813 crore, margin 26.8%, and CDMO revenue above ₹2,000 crore from many customers. The analyst was right to hold management to its numbers each quarter, and management was right about direction, so an investor needs both checks: what was promised and delivered, and what is being built that current numbers do not show.

Deals and orders

Solar Industries will buy South Africa's Omnia Holdings for $1.355 billion (about ₹12,951 crore) in cash. Omnia had FY26 revenue of $1.41 billion, holds net cash, works in 23 countries and owns nitric acid and ammonium nitrate plants, the raw materials for explosives. The deal should close in early to mid 2027. Goldman Sachs said it strengthens Solar's supply chain in Africa. But Solar's shares had risen about 60% in 6 months, touched a 52-week high and then fell 13.64% on 15 September, so the market questioned the price and timing even as it liked the fit. (The fall was confirmed in several market reports.)

eMudhra, a digital signature and security firm, hit its 20% upper limit. Its management targets doubling profit in 3 years. The point made about it: eMudhra owns its own software, while iValue Infosolutions mainly resells other companies' products, so the 2 are not the same business even if both sit under "cybersecurity". Himadri Speciality Chemical is turning around Birla Tyres, which can make about 400 tonnes a day but makes only 80-100; it plans 400 new product types for mining, farm and industrial tyres, 70%+ exports, and a car tyre plant in FY28. Diamond Power Infrastructure won a ₹179.43 crore order for medium-voltage cables from Adani Electricity Mumbai.

07

India: how people are spending

People are buying more even as prices rise, and they are shifting to electric scooters, used phones and credit.

The best festive start in 5 years

Consumer demand in the first part of the festive season, from Onam to Ganesh Chaturthi, is at a 5-year high, even though prices have gone up. For some goods, the Onam-to-Diwali period brings 30% to 35% of the whole year's sales. Maruti Suzuki's retail sales during Onam rose 61%. Electronics makers expect 35% to 40% growth till Diwali and say factories are running at full capacity. LG Electronics called Onam "a very encouraging start".

Apple has raised iPhone prices in India by up to 41%, including older models. Sales of refurbished phones in India rose 13% in the first half of 2026, and Apple phones make up 65.3% of refurbished sales at Cashify. So some buyers are keeping the brand but dropping to a used phone.

India sold about 1.83 lakh electric two-wheelers in August, up 67%. They are now about 10.7% of all two-wheeler sales, against 7.7% a year ago, after crossing 11% in July. Registrations so far in 2026 (1.44 million) have already beaten all of 2025 (1.34 million). Platforms fight over restaurant fees

Flipkart is testing a food delivery app, called "Eat In" internally, with a launch planned in Bengaluru in October. It plans to cap a restaurant's total cost, commission plus ads, at 15% to 20% of the order. Zomato and Swiggy charge 16% to 30% in commission alone, with ad fees on top and no limit. Flipkart's first offer to about 300 restaurants was a 10-11% commission, on the condition that menu prices match dine-in prices. The market is about $9 billion and is expected to reach about $25 billion by FY30. Eternal, which owns Zomato and Blinkit, is selling its AI support tool Nugget to other companies; it handles over 15 million conversations a month, solves 85%+ without a human and cut Zomato's support costs by over $10 million. Amazon Now, Amazon's quick delivery service, has reached $1 billion in yearly gross sales in India.

More Indians are borrowing

74% of Indians eligible for credit had taken some by March, against 35% in March 2017, on a base of 890 million people. First-time borrowers are 13% of new loans, and 46% of their first-quarter loans were for consumer durables such as phones and appliances. Retail loans reached ₹179 lakh crore in the June quarter, up 19.2%. Aditya Birla Housing Finance wants to grow its loan book from ₹48,000 crore to ₹1 lakh crore in 8-10 quarters, is adding at least 100 branches and got ₹2,750 crore from Advent International. It uses 13 live AI tools and has cut its cost to 2.21% of loans from 2.93%, aiming for 1.7%.

What else people are paying for

Milky Mist Dairy grew FY26 revenue 34% to ₹3,138 crore and profit 176% to ₹127 crore. Demand for its yoghurt grows about 50% a year, faster than it can make it, so it will spend ₹400-500 crore over 24-30 months. About 70% of its sales are in South India, and it plans a plant in Maharashtra. Rich Indians are also buying art: Saffronart's Dinesh Vazirani recalls an S.H. Raza work priced at $10,000; Raza's top auction price is now $6.3 million. At the other end, India's fertility rate has fallen below 2.1 children per woman, the level needed to keep a population steady, as the latest official survey data shows. That points to a slower-growing pool of young buyers in the long run.

What each region is choosing

China builds and stockpiles, the West fights its bond markets, and India uses its new weight to broker deals.

China: build, stockpile, wait

People in the chip industry are converging on 2028 to 2031 as the period when China can make chips with EUV lithography, the most advanced chip-printing machines, at high volume. Nvidia chief Jensen Huang said China will get there by 2030 and called 2 or 3 years "just a click". SMIC founder Richard Zhang gives 2028-31. China is also buying about twice as much gold as it reports, and Shanghai crude trading at a record $135 shows China paying up to secure physical oil. Chinese biotech shares have become the newest AI-linked trade. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said China's Belt and Road lending has bankrupted many borrowers, and that people will not use Chinese AI models if the US offers open ones. The pattern is that China is securing inputs, gold, oil and chip tools, even while its factories run on margins as thin as the 3% seen in clothing in section 04.

US, Europe and Japan: fighting their bond markets

Developed countries went from negative interest rates a few years ago to multi-decade highs in bond yields. The US 20-year bond, the UK 30-year gilt at 5.95% and falling European and Japanese bond prices all point the same way. Most EU countries run budget deficits and cannot easily afford higher rates, which is why the European Central Bank is offering foreign central banks cash against euro bonds rather than letting them sell. Bessent is examining a proposal for $5,000 payments to Americans, and the US has pushed countries to pay it back for protecting shipping in the Strait of Hormuz. The UK is considering military support for Saudi Arabia against the Houthis after a request from Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. So the West is spending more on defence and relief exactly when borrowing costs are highest.

India: more weight, used for deals

India hosted the BRICS summit in New Delhi. It was Chinese President Xi Jinping's first visit to India in 7 years, Russian President Vladimir Putin stayed 3 days, and Iran's President Masoud Pezeshkian made his first India visit, also meeting Abu Dhabi Crown Prince Sheikh Khaled. India helped broker an understanding between the UAE and Iran, written into the New Delhi Declaration, and the summit focused on payment systems between member countries rather than a single BRICS currency. India also started the Yudh Abhyas military exercise with the US at Auli and Mahajan in the same period, which shows India keeping ties with both camps.

With Vietnam, India is moving from gifts to joint production. It has supplied 12 high-speed guard boats built by Larsen & Toubro, gifted the warship INS Kirpan, and is now putting $300 million of credit lines ($120 million and $180 million) into making military equipment together. The 2 countries will also work on ports, medicines, nuclear energy and space.

Arguments that are still open

Where informed people disagree, both sides are shown with a way to check who is right.

Should the Fed raise rates into an oil shock?

The case for a hike: inflation is rising again, jet fuel and diesel are climbing, and a Fed that looks soft now could see price rises spread into wages and rents. Goldman Sachs expects a hike, and betting markets put it near 85%. The case against: an oil shock is a supply problem, and higher rates do not make more oil. They cut demand instead, and they land on an economy where mortgages are already at 7.22%, sellers outnumber home buyers by a record 57.9% and New York factory activity just came in at 7.6 against 15.0 expected. Albert Edwards called a hike "criminally stupid". There is also a bond market risk: the 20-year auction showed foreign buyers stepping back, so a hike could push long yields up further and hurt the banks and insurers already holding losses. The way to judge it is to watch whether core inflation (prices without food and fuel) rises in the next 2 reports. If it does, the hike was needed. If only fuel prices rise while core stays flat, the hike mostly adds pain.

Words explained

Every technical term used above, in 1 plain sentence each.

BarrelThe standard unit for crude oil, equal to 159 litres.
Brent and WTIThe 2 main oil price benchmarks: Brent for world crude, WTI for US crude.
Shanghai crudeAn oil futures contract priced for delivery to China.
FuturesA contract to buy or sell something at a set price on a later date.
Strait of HormuzThe channel between Iran and Oman that carries most Gulf oil exports.
Bab el-MandebThe narrow channel at the south end of the Red Sea, between Yemen and Africa.
East-West pipelineA Saudi pipeline that carries crude to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, avoiding Hormuz.
Export banA government order stopping a product from being sold abroad.
Bond yieldThe yearly return a lender demands for lending money for the bond's full term.
Percentage pointThe plain difference between 2 percentages; 5% to 6% is a rise of 1 point.
Auction tailThe extra yield a government pays at a bond sale above what traders expected just before it.
Indirect biddersBuyers at a US bond auction who bid through dealers, mostly foreign institutions.
Yield curve controlA central bank capping long-term yields by printing money to buy bonds.
Repo facilityA way to borrow cash by pledging bonds and buying them back later.
Rate hikeA central bank raising its policy interest rate, which makes loans dearer.
Buyer's marketA housing market with more sellers than buyers, which pushes prices down.
Dollar index (DXY)A measure of the US dollar against a basket of major currencies.
FCNR(B) depositsForeign currency deposits that Indians living abroad keep with Indian banks.
Swap windowAn RBI facility where banks exchange dollars with the RBI now and reverse it later at a set rate.
Forex reservesThe foreign currency and gold a central bank holds to defend its currency.
Open market salesThe RBI selling government bonds to pull surplus cash out of banks.
Repo rateThe rate at which the RBI lends to banks; India's main policy rate, now 5.25%.
MPCThe RBI's Monetary Policy Committee, which sets the repo rate.
Retail inflation (CPI)The yearly rise in prices paid by households.
Core inflationRetail inflation without food and fuel, a guide to lasting price pressure.
WPIWholesale price inflation, the price rise at the factory and trader level.
Gigawatt1,000 megawatts of power capacity.
Gross marginRevenue left after direct costs, as a share of revenue.
